Group rings — Problem Set 1

Definitions and hand computation in ℝ[D₄] and ℝ[Q₈], then left multiplication as a linear operator, then out through Fourier analysis, Galois fields, quadratic reciprocity, and spherical harmonics. 54 problems. 5 August 2026.

On the name. Yes — these are group rings. For a ring R with 1 and a group G, the group ring is written R[G] or RG, coefficients on the outside. So the objects you were pushing around are ℝ[D₄] and ℝ[Q₈], not D₄[ℝ]. When the coefficient ring is a field (as ℝ is), the standard name sharpens to group algebra, since R[G] is then an R-vector space with basis G.

And the general multiplication formula you derived is almost certainly the convolution formula: (αβ)_g = Σ_{hk=g} a_h b_k = Σ_{h∈G} a_h b_{h⁻¹g}. That is the definition, and everything on this page is downstream of it.

Yes, left multiplication is a linear map, and yes, its spectrum is interesting — that is sections E through H, and it turns out to be the thing that explains why ℝ[D₄] ≇ ℝ[Q₈] but ℂ[D₄] ≅ ℂ[Q₈]. Sections J and K are the payoff: the same formalism gives you the Hamming code and quadratic reciprocity.

Conventions used throughout.

  • D₄ is the symmetry group of the square: order 8, presented as ⟨r, s | r⁴ = s² = 1, sr = r⁻¹s⟩. Normal form r^a s^b, 0≤a≤3, 0≤b≤1. Here D_n always means the n-gon group, of order 2n — so D₃ ≅ S₃ and D₂ ≅ C₂×C₂. Dummit & Foote index by order instead and call this same group D₈; if you are reading along in it, halve every subscript.
  • The subscripts in D₄ and Q₈ therefore count different things — D₄ has 4 rotations, Q₈ has 8 elements — and both groups have order 8. That collision is unfortunate and universal; nobody has fixed it.
  • Q₈ = {1, z, i, zi, j, zj, k, zk}, where z is the unique element of order 2 (the thing usually written −1). Rules: z central, z² = 1, i² = j² = k² = z, ij = k, jk = i, ki = j, and ji = zk, kj = zi, ik = zj.
  • Writing z rather than −1 is deliberate; see problem 16.

A. Definitions and first consequences 1–7

#1

1. From memory, write down the full definition of R[G]: underlying set, addition, multiplication. Then say precisely where the finite support condition is needed, by exhibiting what goes wrong if you allow arbitrary functions G → R with G infinite.

Answer
R[G] = functions α: G → R with a_g = 0 for all but finitely many g, written α = Σ_g a_g g. Addition coefficientwise. Multiplication:
(αβ)_g = Σ_{hk = g} a_h b_k = Σ_{h ∈ G} a_h b_{h⁻¹g}
Finite support does two jobs. (i) Each coefficient sum is finite, so the product is defined with no notion of convergence. (ii) The product is again finitely supported: if α lives on S and β on T, then αβ lives inside the finite set ST. Drop it with G = ℤ and α = β = Σ_{n∈ℤ} 1·n: every coefficient of the "product" is an infinite sum of 1's.
#2

2. Prove associativity directly from the formula, by showing that both ((αβ)γ)_g and (α(βγ))_g equal Σ_{uvw = g} a_u b_v c_w. Then check both distributive laws.

Hint
Expand and re-index: ((αβ)γ)_g = Σ_{xw=g} (Σ_{uv=x} a_u b_v) c_w. Pairs (x,w) with xw=g together with factorizations x=uv biject with triples (u,v,w), uvw=g. All sums finite, so reordering is free. That is the whole content: associativity of G lifts to associativity of R[G].
#3

3. Show 1_R·1_G is a two-sided identity. Show g ↦ 1_R·g injects G into the unit group R[G]^×. Show r ↦ r·1_G is an injective ring homomorphism R → R[G], and determine exactly when its image is central.

Answer
All immediate from the formula. r·1_G is central iff r is central in R: group elements commute with it automatically, but (r1_G)(sg) = (rs)g while (sg)(r1_G) = (sr)g. For R = ℝ the scalars are central, so ℝ[G] is an ℝ-algebra.
#4

4. Let R ≠ 0. Prove: R[G] is commutative iff R is commutative and G is abelian. Give the one-line reason neither condition implies the other.

#5

5. For G finite, show dim_ℝ ℝ[G] = |G| with G as basis. State carefully what the linear independence of G inside ℝ[G] is asserting, and why it holds by construction rather than by proof.

Answer
It asserts: Σ a_g g = 0 ⟹ every a_g = 0. True by fiat — an element of ℝ[G] is its coefficient function and the zero element is the zero function. The content is entirely in the construction: we built the free module on the set G. Worth pinning down, because in a representation ρ: ℝ[G] → End(V) the images ρ(g) are generally not independent, and that failure is exactly the kernel you care about.
#6

6. Let C₂ = {1, x}. Show ℝ[C₂] ≅ ℝ × ℝ by writing the isomorphism explicitly on a + bx, and identify the two idempotents giving the splitting.

Answer
a + bx ⟼ (a + b, a − b)
Multiplicative because (a+bx)(c+dx) = (ac+bd) + (ad+bc)x. Idempotents e₊ = (1+x)/2, e₋ = (1−x)/2, with e₊e₋ = 0, e₊+e₋ = 1. This tiny example is the entire later story in miniature.
#7

7. Prove: if G has any element g ≠ 1 of finite order, ℝ[G] has zero divisors. Conclude that for finite G with |G| > 1, ℝ[G] is never a domain, hence never a field.

Answer
With g of order n>1:
(1 − g)(1 + g + g² + ⋯ + g^{n−1}) = 1 − gⁿ = 0
and both factors are nonzero since 1,g,…,g^{n−1} are distinct basis elements. The torsion-free case is the zero-divisor conjecture, open in general — worth knowing it is hard.

B. Hand computation in ℝ[D₄] 8–15

#8

8. List the eight basis elements of ℝ[D₄] in normal form and derive the single reduction rule for (r^a s^b)(r^c s^d).

Answer
Basis 1, r, r², r³, s, rs, r²s, r³s. From sr = r⁻¹s comes s r^c = r^{−c} s, hence
(r^a s^b)(r^c s^d) = r^{a + (−1)^b c} s^{b+d} (a mod 4, b mod 2)
Everything below is this rule applied bilinearly. If you derived a general multiplication formula earlier, this is what it specializes to.
#9

9. Compute (1 + r)(1 + s) and (1 + s)(1 + r). Conclude ℝ[D₄] is not commutative, and note which single term is responsible.

Answer
(1 + r)(1 + s) = 1 + r + s + rs (1 + s)(1 + r) = 1 + r + s + sr = 1 + r + s + r³s
Three of four terms agree; the discrepancy is entirely rs vs r³s.
#10

10. Compute (r + s)², and then (r + s)³.

Answer
(r+s)² = r² + rs + sr + s² = 1 + r² + rs + r³s. For the cube, use (rs)r = r(sr) = r·r³s = s, (rs)s = r, (r³s)r = r³·r³s = r²s, (r³s)s = r³:
(r+s)³ = 2r + 2s + 2r³ + 2r²s
#11

11. Compute (1 + 2r − s)(3 + r³s) in full, in the normal-form basis.

Answer
1·3 = 3; 1·r³s = r³s; 2r·3 = 6r; 2r·r³s = 2r⁴s = 2s; (−s)·3 = −3s; (−s)(r³s) = −(sr³)s = −(rs)s = −r.
(1 + 2r − s)(3 + r³s) = 3 + 5r − s + r³s
#12

12. Let T = 1 + r + r² + r³. (a) Compute T². (b) Show T/4 is idempotent. (c) Compute sT and Ts, concluding T is central. (d) Explain (c) in one sentence without computing.

Answer
(a) Each of 1,r,r²,r³ arises 4 times, so T² = 4T. (b) (T/4)² = 4T/16 = T/4. (c) sT = s + r³s + r²s + rs = Ts. (d) T is the sum over the subgroup ⟨r⟩, which is normal; conjugation permutes a normal subgroup and so fixes its sum. Same argument for any normal subgroup.
#13

13. Let N = Σ_{g∈D₄} g. (a) Show gN = Ng = N for all g. (b) Deduce N² = 8N and that e = N/8 is a central idempotent. (c) Compute αN for general α. (d) Compute (1−e)N.

Answer
(a) Left multiplication by g permutes D₄. (b) N² = Σ_g gN = 8N. (c) αN = (Σ_g a_g)N = ε(α)N — the augmentation shows up on its own. (d) 0, so 1−e and N are zero divisors.
#14

14. (a) Verify (1 − r²)(1 + r²) = 0. (b) Produce a zero-divisor pair not built from ⟨r²⟩. (c) Show (1+r²)/2 is a central idempotent and compute its product with (1−r²)/2.

Answer
(a) 1 − r⁴ = 0. (b) (1−s)(1+s) = 0, or the same with any reflection. (c) r² is central of order 2, so ((1+r²)/2)² = (2+2r²)/4 = (1+r²)/2, and the two halves multiply to (1−r⁴)/4 = 0. Remember this pair — it is the projector that isolates the 2×2 block in #44.
#15

15. Find the centralizer of s in ℝ[D₄]: all α with sα = αs. Give a basis and the dimension.

Hint
Since s² = 1, the condition is sαs = α. Conjugation by s permutes the basis; find the orbits, take fixed points and orbit sums.
Answer
Conjugation by s fixes 1, r², s, r²s and swaps r ↔ r³, rs ↔ r³s. (Check: s(rs)s = (sr)s·s… = sr = r³s.) Basis of the fixed space:
1, r², s, r²s, r + r³, rs + r³s
Dimension 6.

C. Hand computation in ℝ[Q₈] 16–21

#16

16. The notation trap. Let z ∈ Q₈ be the element of order 2, normally written −1. Explain why in ℝ[Q₈] the basis element z is not the ring element −1 = (−1)·1_G. Show 1 + z ≠ 0, and that 1 + z is a zero divisor.

Answer
{1,z,i,zi,j,zj,k,zk} is a basis, so 1+z has coordinate vector (1,1,0,…,0) ≠ 0. If z were the scalar −1, the eight basis elements would collapse to four and ℝ[Q₈] would be 4-dimensional; it is 8-dimensional. And
(1 + z)(1 − z) = 1 − z² = 0
The quotient that does identify z with −1 is ℝ[Q₈]/(1+z), and that quotient is ℍ (#38). This is the single most common place to lose an hour.
#17

17. Compute (1+i)(1+j), (1+j)(1+i), (1+i)², and (1+i)(1−i).

Answer
(1 + i)(1 + j) = 1 + i + j + k (1 + j)(1 + i) = 1 + i + j + zk (1 + i)² = 1 + z + 2i (1 + i)(1 − i) = 1 − i² = 1 − z
The last is not zero, but 1 − z is itself a zero divisor. In ℍ the same expression is 1 − (−1) = 2, a unit. That gap is precisely what the group ring remembers and ℍ forgets.
#18

18. Compute (i + j + k)² in the 8-element basis.

Answer
Nine products, using i²=j²=k²=z, ij=k, ji=zk, jk=i, kj=zi, ki=j, ik=zj:
(i+j+k)² = 3z + (1 + z)(i + j + k)
Sanity check: multiply by e₋ = (1−z)/2 and the second term dies, leaving 3z·e₋ = −3e₋, i.e. "(i+j+k)² = −3", the expected answer in ℍ.
#19

19. Compute (2 + i − zj)(1 + k) in full.

Answer
i·k = zj and (−zj)k = −z(jk) = −zi, so the zj terms cancel:
(2 + i − zj)(1 + k) = 2 + i − zi + 2k
An accident of these coefficients, not a structural fact.
#20

20. Set e₊ = (1+z)/2, e₋ = (1−z)/2. Show each is idempotent, e₊e₋ = 0, e₊+e₋ = 1, both central. Then compute z·e₋ and (i e₋)².

Answer
z central of order 2 gives e_±² = e_± and e₊e₋ = (1−z²)/4 = 0.
z·e₋ = (z − 1)/2 = −e₋ (i e₋)² = i² e₋ = z e₋ = −e₋
Inside the ring ℝ[Q₈]e₋ — whose identity is e₋, not 1 — the element ie₋ squares to minus the identity. That is how ℍ hides inside ℝ[Q₈].
#21

21. Write down the conjugacy classes of Q₈ and of D₄ and their class sums. Verify by hand that C = r + r³ ∈ ℝ[D₄] commutes with both r and s.

Answer
Q₈: {1}, {z}, {i,zi}, {j,zj}, {k,zk}; sums 1, z, i+zi, j+zj, k+zk.
D₄: {1}, {r²}, {r,r³}, {s,r²s}, {rs,r³s}; sums 1, r², r+r³, s+r²s, rs+r³s.
rC = r² + r⁴ = r² + 1 = Cr; sC = r³s + rs = Cs.
Both groups have five classes. Hold onto that for #39.

D. Augmentation, involution, center 22–25

#22

22. Define ε: ℝ[G] → ℝ, Σ a_g g ↦ Σ a_g. (a) Prove it is a ring homomorphism. (b) Show ker ε has basis {g − 1 : g ≠ 1}. (c) Evaluate ε on your answers to #11 and #19.

Answer
(a) ε(αβ) = Σ_g Σ_{hk=g} a_h b_k = (Σ_h a_h)(Σ_k b_k), each pair counted once. (b) Each g−1 is in the kernel and they are independent; conversely Σa_g = 0 ⟹ Σ a_g g = Σ_{g≠1} a_g(g−1). So dim ker ε = |G|−1. (c) ε(3+5r−s+r³s) = 8; ε(2+i−zi+2k) = 4. Cross-check: ε(1+2r−s)·ε(3+r³s) = 2·4 = 8. ✓
#23

23. (a) If α is a unit, show ε(α) ≠ 0. (b) Show the converse fails, using an element from #16. (c) Show cg is a unit for c ≠ 0 ("trivial units").

Answer
(a) αβ = 1 ⟹ ε(α)ε(β) = 1. (b) ε(1+z) = 2 ≠ 0, but 1+z is a zero divisor, hence not a unit. (c) (cg)(c⁻¹g⁻¹) = 1. Whether ℝ[G] has non-trivial units is the interesting question; over ℤ it is a whole subject.
#24

24. Define α* = Σ a_g g⁻¹. (a) Show α** = α and (αβ)* = β*α*. (b) Show the coefficient of 1_G in αα* equals Σ_g a_g². (c) Conclude that over ℝ, αα* = 0 ⟹ α = 0.

Answer
(a) The coefficient of g⁻¹ in (αβ)* is Σ_{hk=g} a_h b_k, and in β*α* it is Σ_{k⁻¹h⁻¹=g⁻¹} b_k a_h — the same sum. (b) Coefficient of 1 in αα* is Σ_{hk⁻¹=1} a_h a_k = Σ_h a_h². (c) A sum of real squares vanishes only if all terms do. This is genuinely a fact about ℝ — it fails over ℂ and over 𝔽₂, and that failure is why Maschke needs a characteristic hypothesis.
#25

25. Prove the class sums form an ℝ-basis of Z(ℝ[G]), so dim Z(ℝ[G]) = number of conjugacy classes. Confirm this gives 5 for both ℝ[D₄] and ℝ[Q₈].

Hint
α is central iff gαg⁻¹ = α for all g ∈ G (testing on basis elements suffices). Conjugation permutes the basis, so α is central iff its coefficient function is constant on conjugacy classes.

E. Left multiplication as a linear map 26–31

You asked whether left multiplication is a linear transformation and whether its spectrum is ever interesting. It is, and it is: what follows is the left regular representation, and by the end of section H its characteristic polynomial will have told you the difference between ℍ and M₂(ℝ).

#26

26. For α ∈ ℝ[G] define L_α: ℝ[G] → ℝ[G], x ↦ αx. (a) Show L_α is ℝ-linear and α ↦ L_α is an injective ring homomorphism ℝ[G] → End_ℝ(ℝ[G]) ≅ M_{|G|}(ℝ). (b) Show that right multiplication R_α is also linear, but α ↦ R_α is an anti-homomorphism. Fix it using the involution of #24.

Answer
(a) Linearity and multiplicativity are the distributive and associative laws. Injective because L_α(1) = α. So every group algebra embeds in a matrix algebra — a version of Cayley's theorem one level up. (b) R_αR_β(x) = (xβ)α = R_{βα}(x). The map α ↦ R_{α*} is an honest homomorphism.
#27

27. In the ordered basis (1, r, r², r³, s, rs, r²s, r³s) of ℝ[D₄], write the matrices of L_r and L_s. Give the cycle structure of each as a permutation, and the characteristic polynomial of each.

Answer
L_r: 1→r→r²→r³→1 and s→rs→r²s→r³s→s. Two 4-cycles; char(L_r) = (t⁴ − 1)²; eigenvalues 1, −1, i, −i each with multiplicity 2.
L_s: 1↔s, r↔r³s, r²↔r²s, r³↔rs. Four transpositions; char(L_s) = (t² − 1)⁴; eigenvalues ±1 each with multiplicity 4.
Note both are permutation matrices — that is what left multiplication by a group element always is.
#28

28. Generalize. For g ∈ G of order n, show L_g is a permutation matrix whose cycles are all of length n, and there are |G|/n of them. Deduce the characteristic polynomial and the full spectrum with multiplicities. Then compute tr(L_g) for every g.

Answer
Left translation is a free action: gx = x ⟹ g = 1. So the orbits of ⟨g⟩ are all of size n, giving |G|/n cycles of length n.
char(L_g) = (t^n − 1)^{|G|/n}, spectrum = {n-th roots of unity}, each with multiplicity |G|/n tr(L_g) = |G| if g = 1, and 0 otherwise
That last line is the regular character. It is the most useful two-case formula in the subject, and you just proved it by counting fixed points of a permutation.
#29

29. Deduce that tr(L_α) = |G|·a_1 for α = Σ a_g g. Then show ⟨α, β⟩ := (1/|G|)·tr(L_{αβ*}) equals Σ_g a_g b_g, so the regular representation reconstructs the standard inner product on ℝ[G].

#30

30. Spectra of the idempotents you have already built. (a) If e² = e, show L_e is a projection, so its only eigenvalues are 0 and 1 and rank(L_e) = dim ℝ[G]e. (b) Compute the rank of L_e for e = N/8 in ℝ[D₄]. (c) Compute the rank of L_{e₋} for e₋ = (1−z)/2 in ℝ[Q₈]. (d) Compute the spectrum of L_N.

Answer
(b) ℝ[D₄]e = ℝe since ge = e for all g; rank 1. (c) ℝ[Q₈]e₋ has basis e₋, ie₋, je₋, ke₋; rank 4 — and by #20 that 4-dimensional space is ℍ. (d) N² = 8N, so L_N satisfies t² = 8t: eigenvalue 8 with multiplicity 1, eigenvalue 0 with multiplicity 7.
#31

31. Show that if α is central then L_α commutes with every L_β and with every R_β. Explain in one sentence why this forces L_α to act as a scalar on each irreducible block of the decomposition you will build in section G.

Hint
Schur. An operator commuting with an irreducible action, over an algebraically closed field, is a scalar. Over ℝ it can also be multiplication by a complex or quaternionic unit — which is exactly the gap between #38 and #39.

F. The spectrum is a Fourier transform 32–35

Here is the harmonic-analysis valence you were hoping for. It is not an analogy: for abelian G, "diagonalize left multiplication" and "take the Fourier transform" are the same sentence.

#32

32. Let C_n = ⟨x⟩ and α = Σ_{j} a_j x^j. Show the matrix of L_α in the basis (1, x, …, x^{n−1}) is the circulant matrix with (i,k) entry a_{i−k mod n}.

#33

33. Work over ℂ, let ω = e^{2πi/n}, and set f_m = Σ_{j=0}^{n−1} ω^{−mj} x^j. (a) Show x·f_m = ω^m f_m. (b) Deduce α·f_m = α̂(m)·f_m where α̂(m) = Σ_j a_j ω^{mj}. (c) Conclude: the spectrum of L_α is exactly the discrete Fourier transform of its coefficient sequence, and det L_α = Π_{m} α̂(m).

Answer
(a) x f_m = Σ_j ω^{−mj}x^{j+1} = Σ_l ω^{−m(l−1)}x^l = ω^m f_m. (b) α f_m = Σ_j a_j x^j f_m = (Σ_j a_j ω^{mj}) f_m. (c) The f_m are n independent eigenvectors, so L_α is diagonalizable with those eigenvalues; the determinant is their product. This is the classical circulant determinant formula, and it is a one-line consequence of group-ring thinking.
#34

34. (a) Show e_m = f_m/n are orthogonal primitive idempotents with Σ_m e_m = 1. (b) Conclude ℂ[C_n] ≅ ℂⁿ via α ↦ (α̂(0), …, α̂(n−1)), and state what this map does to a product. (c) Write out all four idempotents for n = 4 explicitly.

Answer
(a) f_m f_l = (Σ_j ω^{(l−m)j}) f_l = n δ_{ml} f_l. (b) It is a ring isomorphism carrying convolution to pointwise multiplication — the convolution theorem, which here is just "the group ring of a finite abelian group splits along its characters." (c) With ω = i: e_m = ¼(1 + i^{−m}x + i^{−2m}x² + i^{−3m}x³) for m = 0,1,2,3.
#35

35. Now do it over ℝ instead of ℂ, for n = 4. Show ℝ[C₄] ≅ ℝ × ℝ × ℂ, and explain precisely which two of the four complex eigenvalues glue together and why.

Answer
ℝ[C₄] ≅ ℝ[t]/(t⁴−1) = ℝ[t]/(t−1) × ℝ[t]/(t+1) × ℝ[t]/(t²+1) ≅ ℝ × ℝ × ℂ. The real idempotents are (1+x+x²+x³)/4, (1−x+x²−x³)/4, and (1−x²)/2. The characters χ₁ and χ₃ are complex conjugates; over ℝ you cannot separate a character from its conjugate, so their two ℂ-lines fuse into one 2-dimensional real block that is ℂ. Dimensions 1+1+2 = 4. ✓
Keep this mechanism in mind: "ℝ cannot separate conjugates" is the entire reason section H comes out the way it does.

G. Semisimple structure 36–39

#36

36. State Maschke's theorem and Artin–Wedderburn. For each, identify exactly which hypothesis is doing the work, and where ℝ vs ℂ vs 𝔽₂ matters.

Answer
Maschke: G finite and char(k) ∤ |G| ⟹ k[G] semisimple. The hypothesis is that |G| is invertible in k; the averaging idempotent N/|G| of #13 is the entire proof. Over 𝔽₂, 𝔽₂[D₄] is not semisimple — it is local, with nilpotent radical.
Artin–Wedderburn: a semisimple ring is a finite product Π M_{n_i}(D_i) over division rings. Over ℂ the only finite-dimensional division algebra is ℂ, so you get pure matrix rings. Over ℝ they are ℝ, ℂ, ℍ (Frobenius) — that extra room is what lets ℝ[D₄] and ℝ[Q₈] differ.
#37

37. Using e₊, e₋ from #20: show ℝ[Q₈] ≅ ℝ[Q₈]e₊ × ℝ[Q₈]e₋, identify the first factor with ℝ[Q₈/⟨z⟩] = ℝ[C₂×C₂] ≅ ℝ⁴, and the second with ℍ. Check 4 + 4 = 8.

Answer
Central orthogonal idempotents summing to 1 always split a ring as a direct product. On the e₊ side z e₊ = e₊, so z acts as the identity and the factor is ℝ[Q₈/⟨z⟩], with quotient the Klein four group and ℝ[C₂×C₂] ≅ ℝ⁴. On the e₋ side ze₋ = −e₋, so the images of i,j,k square to minus the identity and anticommute: basis e₋, ie₋, je₋, ke₋, which is ℍ.
ℝ[Q₈] ≅ ℝ × ℝ × ℝ × ℝ × ℍ
#38

38. Do the same for ℝ[D₄], using the idempotent from #14.

Answer
ℝ[D₄] ≅ ℝ × ℝ × ℝ × ℝ × M₂(ℝ)
#39

39. Compare #37 and #38 and say precisely what goes wrong with the guess: "same order, same number of conjugacy classes, same center dimension, therefore isomorphic."

Answer
Every numerical invariant you have computed agrees: dimension 8, four 1-dimensional blocks and one 4-dimensional block, 5-dimensional center. But M₂(ℝ) has zero divisors and ℍ is a division ring, so the two are not isomorphic. Concretely, 1 − s is a zero divisor sitting in ℝ[D₄]'s 4-dimensional block, and ℝ[Q₈] has nothing like it there.

The sharp version, which is #44: over ℂ the two are isomorphic. D₄ and Q₈ have identical character tables. Real coefficients see a difference complex coefficients cannot — the Frobenius–Schur indicator.

H. The group determinant, and one quadratic form that explains everything 40–43

This section is where left multiplication earns its keep. In 1896 Dedekind computed the determinant below for abelian groups, watched it split into linear factors, tried a nonabelian group, watched it fail to split, and wrote to Frobenius. Character theory is what came back.

J. Group rings over a Galois field: cyclic codes 45–48

Change the coefficient ring from ℝ to 𝔽_q and the same object becomes the foundation of algebraic coding theory. Every ingredient you have — ideals, idempotents, characters, the Fourier transform — reappears, now with Frobenius doing the bookkeeping.

Answer
The shift is left multiplication by the generator x, i.e. L_x. A subspace closed under L_x is closed under L_{p(x)} for every polynomial, which is every element of the ring. Since the ring is commutative, that is exactly an ideal. And ideals of 𝔽_q[X]/(Xⁿ−1) correspond to divisors of Xⁿ−1 — so classifying cyclic codes is factoring Xⁿ−1.

K. Capstone: Gauss sums, cyclotomy, quadratic reciprocity 49

One object, three rooms. The Gauss sum is an element of a group ring; its square is a harmonic-analysis computation; the Galois group acts on it; and reducing it modulo a second prime — that is, moving to a group ring over a Galois field — gives quadratic reciprocity. This is the standard three-page proof, and every step is elementary. Take your time.

#49

49. Multi-part. Let p be an odd prime, ζ = e^{2πi/p}, and (a|p) the Legendre symbol. juicy

(a) Show the map ℤ[C_p] → ℤ[ζ], x ↦ ζ, is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel generated by N = 1 + x + ⋯ + x^{p−1}. So ℤ[ζ] is a group ring modulo one relation, and every identity below is really a group-ring identity.

(b) Define the quadratic Gauss sum g = Σ_{a=1}^{p−1} (a|p)·ζ^a. Explain why this is the Fourier transform of the quadratic character evaluated at 1, and why that makes it the natural bridge between the multiplicative and additive structures of 𝔽_p.

(c) Prove Σ_{a=0}^{p−1} ζ^{ac} = p if c ≡ 0 and 0 otherwise. Then prove g·ḡ = p.

Hint
In gḡ = Σ_{a,b}(ab|p)ζ^{a−b}, substitute a = bc and let b range. The (b²|p) that appears is 1.

(d) Show ḡ = (−1|p)·g, and conclude

g² = (−1)^{(p−1)/2}·p =: p*

(e) Galois. For σ_a ∈ Gal(ℚ(ζ)/ℚ) ≅ (ℤ/p)^× defined by ζ ↦ ζ^a, show σ_a(g) = (a|p)·g. Deduce that ℚ(g) = ℚ(√{p*}) is the unique quadratic subfield of ℚ(ζ_p), and say why "unique" is automatic.

Hint
(ℤ/p)^× is cyclic. How many subgroups of index 2 does a cyclic group have?

(f) Reduce mod a second prime. Let q ≠ p be an odd prime and work in ℤ[ζ]/qℤ[ζ] — a group ring over a field of characteristic q. Prove g^q ≡ (q|p)·g two different ways: once using the Frobenius y ↦ y^q, once using g² = p* together with Euler's criterion. Compare the two answers.

Hint
In characteristic q, raising a sum to the q-th power is additive — that is the only property of the Frobenius you need. For the second route, g^q = g·(g²)^{(q−1)/2}.

(g) Conclude quadratic reciprocity: for distinct odd primes p, q,

(p|q)(q|p) = (−1)^{((p−1)/2)((q−1)/2)}

Answers
(a) Surjective since ζ generates. N ↦ 1 + ζ + ⋯ + ζ^{p−1} = 0, and ℤ[C_p]/(N) ≅ ℤ[X]/(X^p−1, 1+X+⋯+X^{p−1}) = ℤ[X]/Φ_p(X) ≅ ℤ[ζ].

(b) a ↦ (a|p) is the unique nontrivial multiplicative character of 𝔽_p^×; a ↦ ζ^a is an additive character. g pairs one against the other. Gauss sums are exactly the change-of-basis coefficients between additive and multiplicative harmonic analysis on 𝔽_p — which is why they show up everywhere from Dirichlet L-functions to the Weil conjectures.

(c) Geometric series: Σ_a ζ^{ac} = (ζ^{pc}−1)/(ζ^c−1) = 0 for c ≢ 0. Then
gḡ = Σ_{a,b}(ab|p)ζ^{a−b} (a = bc) = Σ_{b,c}(b²c|p)ζ^{b(c−1)} = Σ_c (c|p) Σ_b ζ^{b(c−1)} = (1|p)(p−1) − Σ_{c≠1}(c|p) = (p−1) − (0 − 1) = p
(using Σ_{c}(c|p) = 0).

(d) ḡ = Σ_a (a|p)ζ^{−a} = Σ_b (−b|p)ζ^{b} = (−1|p)g. So (−1|p)g² = p, and since (−1|p) = ±1, g² = (−1|p)p = (−1)^{(p−1)/2}p = p*.

(e) σ_a(g) = Σ_b (b|p)ζ^{ab} = Σ_c (a⁻¹c|p)ζ^{c} = (a|p)g since (a⁻¹|p) = (a|p). So g is fixed exactly by σ_a with a a quadratic residue — an index-2 subgroup — hence ℚ(g) is a quadratic extension of ℚ, and g² = p* makes it ℚ(√p*). Uniqueness: Gal(ℚ(ζ_p)/ℚ) ≅ (ℤ/p)^× is cyclic of order p−1, and a cyclic group has exactly one subgroup of each order dividing the order — so exactly one index-2 subgroup, so exactly one quadratic subfield.
The quadratic subfield of ℚ(ζ_p) is ℚ(√((−1)^{(p−1)/2}·p)).
That is a complete, sharp, nonobvious theorem, and you got it out of a nine-line computation in a group ring.

(f) Route 1: in characteristic q, g^q ≡ Σ_a (a|p)^q ζ^{aq} = Σ_a (a|p)ζ^{aq} = σ_q(g) = (q|p)g, using (a|p)^q = (a|p) for odd q.
Route 2: g^q = g(g²)^{(q−1)/2} = g(p*)^{(q−1)/2} ≡ g·(p*|q) by Euler's criterion.
So (q|p)g ≡ (p*|q)g (mod q). Multiply by g: (q|p)p* ≡ (p*|q)p*, and p* is invertible mod q, so (q|p) ≡ (p*|q) (mod q). Both sides are ±1 and q > 2, so they are equal.

(g) (p*|q) = ((−1)^{(p−1)/2}|q)(p|q) = (−1)^{((p−1)/2)((q−1)/2)}(p|q), using (−1|q) = (−1)^{(q−1)/2}. Combined with (q|p) = (p*|q):
(q|p) = (−1)^{((p−1)/2)((q−1)/2)}·(p|q) ⟺ (p|q)(q|p) = (−1)^{((p−1)/2)((q−1)/2)}
Gauss's theorema aureum, proved by writing down one element of a group ring, squaring it, and reducing it modulo a prime.

L. The corridor: group rings → Laplacians → manifolds and harmonics 50–54

You called it a corridor and that is the right word. Here is the load-bearing sentence: a group ring is the algebra of translation-invariant operators, and a Laplacian is a translation-invariant operator. Everything from the n-cycle to spherical harmonics is that one observation at different levels of generality. Problems 51 and 52 are hand computations; 53 and 54 are the doorway.

#50

50. Reinterpret ℂ[G] as the space of functions G → ℂ under convolution. Prove: a linear operator T on ℂ[G] commutes with every right translation R_h if and only if T = L_α for a unique α. So ℂ[G] is the algebra of translation-invariant operators.

Answer
(⟸) is associativity. (⟹) Put α = T(1). Then for any h, T(h) = T(R_h 1) = R_h T(1) = αh, and T = L_α by linearity. Uniqueness because L_α(1) = α.

This one lemma is the corridor. Replace "finite group + counting measure" by "compact group + Haar measure" and it still holds; replace it by "ℝⁿ under translation" and translation-invariant operators are exactly convolution operators, i.e. Fourier multipliers — which is why constant-coefficient differential operators (the Laplacian foremost) are diagonalized by the Fourier transform.
#51

51. The discrete Laplacian is a group-ring element. In ℝ[C_n], set Δ = 2·1 − x − x^{n−1}. (a) Show L_Δ is the graph Laplacian of the n-cycle. (b) Compute its full spectrum using #33. Answer
(a) (Δ·f)(j) = 2f(j) − f(j−1) − f(j+1), the second-difference operator on the cycle. (b) By #33 the eigenvalue at frequency m is
λ_m = 2 − ω^m − ω^{−m} = 2 − 2cos(2πm/n) = 4sin²(πm/n)
(c) λ_0 = 0 with eigenvector the constant function — ker Δ is the constants, one dimension, reflecting that the cycle is connected. For 0 < m < n/2, λ_m = λ_{n−m}: eigenvalues come in pairs, because the characters χ_m and χ_{n−m} are complex conjugates and cannot be separated over ℝ. Those pairs are exactly the 2-dimensional real blocks of #35, and over ℝ their eigenvectors are cos(2πmj/n) and sin(2πmj/n).
(d) With h = 2π/n, the rescaled eigenvalue is h^{−2}λ_m = h^{−2}(2 − 2cos(mh)), and expanding the cosine gives h^{−2}(m²h² + O(h⁴)) → m² as n → ∞. The eigenvalues of −d²/dθ² on L²(S¹) are m² with eigenfunctions e^{imθ}. The discrete Fourier transform of #33 converges to the Fourier series, and the group ring converges to the convolution algebra of the circle.

Look at what happened: you computed the spectrum of an 8-vertex graph without ever writing down an 8×8 matrix, by factoring the problem through the representation theory. This is the whole method behind expander constructions and Ramanujan graphs.

For G = S¹: characters are e_m(θ) = e^{imθ}, and f * e_m = f̂(m)·e_m — every convolution operator is diagonal in the character basis, with eigenvalue the Fourier coefficient. That is #33 word for word, with Σ_j replaced by ∫dθ. All the irreducibles are 1-dimensional because S¹ is abelian, so the direct sum is over ℤ and the blocks are 1×1.

The Peter–Weyl theorem is exactly the statement that the finite-dimensional row survives the limit: L²(G) for compact G decomposes as a Hilbert direct sum of n_π copies of each irreducible V_π, matrix-coefficient by matrix-coefficient. 54. The far end of the corridor: spherical harmonics. Let S² = SO(3)/SO(2).

(a) Verify that x, y, z are harmonic homogeneous polynomials on ℝ³, that SO(3) preserves their span, and that their restrictions to S² satisfy Δ_{S²}Y = −2Y.

(b) Let H_ℓ = harmonic homogeneous polynomials of degree ℓ in 3 variables. Compute dim H_ℓ for ℓ = 0,1,2,3 by hand, and prove dim H_ℓ = 2ℓ+1 in general.

(c) State why Δ_{S²} acts as a scalar on each H_ℓ, citing the exact problem on this page that is doing the work.

(d) L²(S²) = ⊕̂_{ℓ≥0} H_ℓ, and H_ℓ is the (2ℓ+1)-dimensional irreducible representation of SO(3). Explain why every odd-dimensional irreducible appears exactly once, and why the even-dimensional ones do not appear at all.

Hint
(c) It is #31, verbatim, for a compact group: an operator commuting with an irreducible action is a scalar. Which action does Δ_{S²} commute with, and why? (d) Functions on G/K are functions on G invariant under right K-translation. So an irreducible V_π contributes dim V_π^K copies. Then ask what SO(2)-fixed vectors look like in each SO(3)-irrep, and remember that the even-dimensional ones are honest representations of SU(2) but not of SO(3).
Answer
(a) Δ x = 0 trivially; SO(3) acts by linear substitution, preserving degree, and commutes with Δ because Δ is rotation-invariant. For a harmonic homogeneous p of degree ℓ, separating variables in Δ = ∂_r² + (2/r)∂_r + r^{−2}Δ_{S²} applied to r^ℓ Y gives Δ_{S²}Y = −ℓ(ℓ+1)Y; at ℓ = 1 that is −2.
(b) Homogeneous degree-ℓ polynomials in 3 variables have dimension C(ℓ+2, 2), and Δ maps them onto degree ℓ−2, so
dim H_ℓ = C(ℓ+2,2) − C(ℓ,2) = 2ℓ+1
giving 1, 3, 5, 7 for ℓ = 0,1,2,3.
(c) #31. Δ_{S²} commutes with the SO(3)-action (rotation invariance), so it is an intertwiner of an irreducible representation, so by Schur it is a scalar. In group-ring language: Δ is a central element of the convolution algebra, and central elements act by scalars on each isotypic block — exactly as N did in #30(d) and as the class sums do in #25.
(d) L²(S²) = L²(SO(3)/SO(2)) = L²(SO(3))^{SO(2)}, so by Peter–Weyl each irreducible V_π appears with multiplicity dim V_π^{SO(2)}. In the (2ℓ+1)-dimensional irrep the SO(2)-weights are −ℓ, …, 0, …, ℓ: exactly one zero weight, so exactly one fixed vector, so multiplicity one. The even-dimensional irreps have half-integer weights, no zero weight, no fixed vector — and in fact they are representations of SU(2) that do not descend to SO(3) at all. Multiplicity zero.

So: the decomposition of L²(S²) into spherical harmonics, the eigenvalues −ℓ(ℓ+1), and the degeneracy 2ℓ+1 of each energy level — the structure that makes the periodic table have the shape it has — is the same theorem as ℂ[D₄] ≅ ℂ⁴ × M₂(ℂ). One is finite and you can do it by hand in an evening; the other is compact and needs an integral. The algebra is identical.

Where the three rooms meet. The through-line of this page is a single move: a group ring turns a group into a linear-algebra problem, and its natural coordinates are characters. In section F those characters are the DFT and the coordinates diagonalize convolution. In section H the failure to fully diagonalize over ℝ is a quadratic form, and the classification of quadratic forms is the classification of the algebra. In section J the characters live in a Galois field and the Frobenius orbits control everything. In section K the character is the Legendre symbol, its Fourier coefficient is a Gauss sum, the Galois group acts on that sum by that same character, and reduction mod q closes the loop.

And in section L the group is no longer finite: the same characters become Fourier modes, the same central elements become Laplacians, and the same block decomposition becomes Peter–Weyl. The corridor you named runs the whole length of the page, and #50 is its hinge.

If you want the next rung: |g| = √p is the prototype of square-root cancellation, the phenomenon the Weil conjectures generalize; Tao's uncertainty principle over ℤ/p (#48c) is proved by a Galois-theoretic fact about vanishing sums of roots of unity; and the ℂ[G] → C^*(G) completion of #50 is the doorway into noncommutative geometry, where "manifold" is replaced by the algebra outright. All of these are group-ring statements wearing different hats.
