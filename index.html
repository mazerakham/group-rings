On the name. These are group rings. For a ring $R$ with $1$ and a group $G$, the group ring is written $R[G]$ or $RG$, coefficients on the outside. When the coefficient ring is a field (as $\mathbb{R}$ is), the standard name sharpens to group algebra, since $R[G]$ is then an $R$-vector space with basis $G$.

The general multiplication formula is the convolution formula, $$(\alpha\beta)_g \;=\; \sum_{hk=g} a_h b_k \;=\; \sum_{h\in G} a_h\, b_{h^{-1}g},$$ and everything on this page is downstream of it.

Sections A–D are chapter-opener exercises. E–H develop left multiplication as a linear operator, ending in the one quadratic form that explains why $\mathbb{R}[D_4]\not\cong\mathbb{R}[Q_8]$ while $\mathbb{C}[D_4]\cong\mathbb{C}[Q_8]$. J–L are the payoff: the same formalism produces the Hamming code, quadratic reciprocity, and the decomposition of $L^2(S^2)$.

Conventions.

  • $D_n$ is the symmetry group of the regular $n$-gon, of order $2n$. So $D_4$ is the group of the square, order $8$, presented as $\langle r,s \mid r^4=s^2=1,\; sr=r^{-1}s\rangle$, with normal form $r^as^b$ for $0\le a\le 3$, $0\le b\le 1$. In this convention $D_3\cong S_3$ and $D_2\cong C_2\times C_2$. Dummit & Foote index by order and call this same group $D_8$; halve every subscript if you are reading along in it.
  • $Q_8=\{1,z,i,zi,j,zj,k,zk\}$, where $z$ is the unique element of order $2$ (the thing usually written $-1$). Rules: $z$ central, $z^2=1$, $i^2=j^2=k^2=z$, $ij=k$, $jk=i$, $ki=j$, and $ji=zk$, $kj=zi$, $ik=zj$.
  • Writing $z$ rather than $-1$ is deliberate; see problem 16.
  • $D_4$ and $Q_8$ both have order $8$ despite the subscripts, because the two families index different things.
  • $A\times B$ always denotes the direct product ring: pairs $(a,b)$ with componentwise addition and multiplication, $(a,b)(a',b')=(aa',bb')$, identity $(1_A,1_B)$. Never a tensor product, never a field extension. Note that any such product has zero divisors, since $(1,0)(0,1)=(0,0)$, so a statement like $\mathbb{R}[C_2]\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ is asserting a good deal more than a dimension count. We write $\mathbb{R}^n$ for the $n$-fold product $\mathbb{R}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}$ when it is being used as a ring.

A. Definitions and first consequences 1–7

#1

1. From memory, write down the full definition of $R[G]$: underlying set, addition, multiplication. Then say precisely where the finite support condition is needed, by exhibiting what goes wrong if you allow arbitrary functions $G\to R$ with $G$ infinite.

Answer

$R[G]$ is the set of functions $\alpha:G\to R$ with $a_g=0$ for all but finitely many $g$, written $\alpha=\sum_g a_g g$. Addition is coefficientwise. Multiplication is $$(\alpha\beta)_g=\sum_{hk=g}a_hb_k=\sum_{h\in G}a_h b_{h^{-1}g}.$$

Finite support does two jobs. First, each coefficient sum is finite, so the product is defined with no notion of convergence. Second, the product is again finitely supported: if $\alpha$ lives on $S$ and $\beta$ on $T$, then $\alpha\beta$ lives inside the finite set $ST$. Drop it with $G=\mathbb{Z}$ and $\alpha=\beta=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}1\cdot n$: every coefficient of the "product" is an infinite sum of $1$'s.

#2

2. Prove associativity directly from the formula, by showing that both $((\alpha\beta)\gamma)_g$ and $(\alpha(\beta\gamma))_g$ equal $\sum_{uvw=g}a_ub_vc_w$. Then check both distributive laws.

Hint

Expand and re-index: $$((\alpha\beta)\gamma)_g=\sum_{xw=g}\Big(\sum_{uv=x}a_ub_v\Big)c_w.$$ Pairs $(x,w)$ with $xw=g$ together with factorizations $x=uv$ biject with triples $(u,v,w)$ satisfying $uvw=g$. All sums are finite, so reordering is free. That is the whole content: associativity of $G$ lifts to associativity of $R[G]$.

#3

3. Show $1_R\cdot 1_G$ is a two-sided identity. Show $g\mapsto 1_R\cdot g$ injects $G$ into the unit group $R[G]^\times$. Show $r\mapsto r\cdot 1_G$ is an injective ring homomorphism $R\to R[G]$, and determine exactly when its image is central.

Answer

All immediate from the formula. The element $r\cdot 1_G$ is central if and only if $r$ is central in $R$: group elements commute with it automatically, but $(r1_G)(sg)=(rs)g$ while $(sg)(r1_G)=(sr)g$. For $R=\mathbb{R}$ the scalars are central, so $\mathbb{R}[G]$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-algebra.

#4

4. Let $R\neq 0$. Prove that $R[G]$ is commutative if and only if $R$ is commutative and $G$ is abelian. Give the one-line reason neither condition implies the other.

#5

5. For $G$ finite, show $\dim_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{R}[G]=|G|$ with $G$ as basis. State carefully what the linear independence of $G$ inside $\mathbb{R}[G]$ is asserting, and why it holds by construction rather than by proof.

Answer

It asserts that $\sum_g a_gg=0$ forces every $a_g=0$. This is true by fiat: an element of $\mathbb{R}[G]$ is its coefficient function, and the zero element is the zero function. The content is entirely in the construction, since we built the free module on the set $G$.

Worth pinning down, because in a representation $\rho:\mathbb{R}[G]\to\operatorname{End}(V)$ the images $\rho(g)$ are generally not independent, and that failure is exactly the kernel you care about.

#6

6. Let $C_2=\{1,x\}$. Show $\mathbb{R}[C_2]\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ by writing the isomorphism explicitly on $a+bx$, and identify the two idempotents giving the splitting.

Here $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ is the componentwise product ring: $(u,v)+(u',v')=(u+u',v+v')$ and $(u,v)(u',v')=(uu',vv')$, with identity $(1,1)$. So you are being asked to exhibit coordinates on $\mathbb{R}[C_2]$ in which multiplication becomes componentwise. Note this is not $\mathbb{C}$, which would need $x^2=-1$; same dimension, different ring. An idempotent is an element with $e^2=e$; you can find the two you want either by transporting $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ back through your isomorphism, or directly by setting $e=a+bx$ and solving $e^2=e$.

Answer

$$a+bx\;\longmapsto\;(a+b,\;a-b).$$ It is multiplicative because $(a+bx)(c+dx)=(ac+bd)+(ad+bc)x$. The idempotents are $$e_+=\tfrac{1+x}{2},\qquad e_-=\tfrac{1-x}{2},\qquad e_+e_-=0,\quad e_++e_-=1.$$ This tiny example is the entire later story in miniature.

#7

7. Prove that if $G$ has any element $g\neq 1$ of finite order, then $\mathbb{R}[G]$ has zero divisors. Conclude that for finite $G$ with $|G|>1$, $\mathbb{R}[G]$ is never a domain, hence never a field.

Answer

With $g$ of order $n>1$, $$(1-g)(1+g+g^2+\cdots+g^{n-1})=1-g^n=0,$$ and both factors are nonzero since $1,g,\dots,g^{n-1}$ are distinct basis elements. The torsion-free case is the zero-divisor conjecture, open in general; worth knowing that it is hard.

B. Hand computation in $\mathbb{R}[D_4]$ 8–15

#8

8. List the eight basis elements of $\mathbb{R}[D_4]$ in normal form and derive the single reduction rule for $(r^as^b)(r^cs^d)$.

Answer

Basis $1,r,r^2,r^3,s,rs,r^2s,r^3s$. From $sr=r^{-1}s$ comes $sr^c=r^{-c}s$, hence $$(r^as^b)(r^cs^d)=r^{\,a+(-1)^bc}\,s^{\,b+d}\qquad(a\bmod 4,\;b\bmod 2).$$ Everything below is this rule applied bilinearly.

#9

9. Compute $(1+r)(1+s)$ and $(1+s)(1+r)$. Conclude $\mathbb{R}[D_4]$ is not commutative, and note which single term is responsible.

Answer

$$\begin{aligned} (1+r)(1+s)&=1+r+s+rs\\ (1+s)(1+r)&=1+r+s+sr=1+r+s+r^3s \end{aligned}$$ Three of four terms agree; the discrepancy is entirely $rs$ against $r^3s$.

#10

10. Compute $(r+s)^2$, and then $(r+s)^3$.

Answer

$(r+s)^2=r^2+rs+sr+s^2=1+r^2+rs+r^3s$. For the cube, use $(rs)r=r(sr)=r\cdot r^3s=s$, $(rs)s=r$, $(r^3s)r=r^3\cdot r^3s=r^2s$, and $(r^3s)s=r^3$: $$(r+s)^3=2r+2s+2r^3+2r^2s.$$

#11

11. Compute $(1+2r-s)(3+r^3s)$ in full, in the normal-form basis.

Answer

Term by term: $1\cdot 3=3$; $1\cdot r^3s=r^3s$; $2r\cdot 3=6r$; $2r\cdot r^3s=2r^4s=2s$; $(-s)\cdot 3=-3s$; and $(-s)(r^3s)=-(sr^3)s=-(rs)s=-r$. So $$(1+2r-s)(3+r^3s)=3+5r-s+r^3s.$$

#12

12. Let $T=1+r+r^2+r^3$. (a) Compute $T^2$. (b) Show $T/4$ is idempotent. (c) Compute $sT$ and $Ts$, concluding $T$ is central. (d) Explain (c) in one sentence without computing.

Answer

(a) Each of $1,r,r^2,r^3$ arises four times, so $T^2=4T$. (b) $(T/4)^2=4T/16=T/4$. (c) $sT=s+r^3s+r^2s+rs=Ts$. (d) $T$ is the sum over the subgroup $\langle r\rangle$, which is normal; conjugation permutes a normal subgroup and so fixes its sum. The same argument works for any normal subgroup.

#13

13. Let $N=\sum_{g\in D_4}g$. (a) Show $gN=Ng=N$ for all $g$. (b) Deduce $N^2=8N$ and that $e=N/8$ is a central idempotent. (c) Compute $\alpha N$ for general $\alpha$. (d) Compute $(1-e)N$.

Answer

(a) Left multiplication by $g$ permutes $D_4$. (b) $N^2=\sum_g gN=8N$. (c) $\alpha N=\big(\sum_g a_g\big)N=\varepsilon(\alpha)N$, so the augmentation shows up on its own. (d) $(1-e)N=0$, so $1-e$ and $N$ are zero divisors.

#14

14. (a) Verify $(1-r^2)(1+r^2)=0$. (b) Produce a zero-divisor pair not built from $\langle r^2\rangle$. (c) Show $(1+r^2)/2$ is a central idempotent and compute its product with $(1-r^2)/2$.

Answer

(a) $1-r^4=0$. (b) $(1-s)(1+s)=0$, or the same with any reflection. (c) $r^2$ is central of order $2$, so $\big(\tfrac{1+r^2}{2}\big)^2=\tfrac{2+2r^2}{4}=\tfrac{1+r^2}{2}$, and the two halves multiply to $\tfrac{1-r^4}{4}=0$.

Remember this pair. It is the projector that isolates the $2\times 2$ block in #44.

#15

15. Find the centralizer of $s$ in $\mathbb{R}[D_4]$: all $\alpha$ with $s\alpha=\alpha s$. Give a basis and the dimension.

Hint

Since $s^2=1$, the condition is $s\alpha s=\alpha$. Conjugation by $s$ permutes the basis; find the orbits, then take fixed points and orbit sums.

Answer

Conjugation by $s$ fixes $1,r^2,s,r^2s$ and swaps $r\leftrightarrow r^3$ and $rs\leftrightarrow r^3s$. (Check one: $s(rs)s=(sr)(ss)=sr=r^3s$.) A basis of the fixed space is $$1,\quad r^2,\quad s,\quad r^2s,\quad r+r^3,\quad rs+r^3s,$$ so the dimension is $\mathbf{6}$.

C. Hand computation in $\mathbb{R}[Q_8]$ 16–21

#16

16. The notation trap. Let $z\in Q_8$ be the element of order $2$, normally written $-1$. Explain why in $\mathbb{R}[Q_8]$ the basis element $z$ is not the ring element $-1=(-1)\cdot 1_G$. Show $1+z\neq 0$, and that $1+z$ is a zero divisor.

Answer

$\{1,z,i,zi,j,zj,k,zk\}$ is a basis, so $1+z$ has coordinate vector $(1,1,0,\dots,0)\neq 0$. If $z$ were the scalar $-1$, the eight basis elements would collapse to four and $\mathbb{R}[Q_8]$ would be $4$-dimensional; it is $8$-dimensional. And $$(1+z)(1-z)=1-z^2=0.$$ The quotient that does identify $z$ with $-1$ is $\mathbb{R}[Q_8]/(1+z)$, and that quotient is $\mathbb{H}$ (see #37). This is the single most common place to lose an hour.

#17

17. Compute $(1+i)(1+j)$, $(1+j)(1+i)$, $(1+i)^2$, and $(1+i)(1-i)$.

Answer

$$\begin{aligned} (1+i)(1+j)&=1+i+j+k\\ (1+j)(1+i)&=1+i+j+zk\\ (1+i)^2&=1+z+2i\\ (1+i)(1-i)&=1-i^2=1-z \end{aligned}$$ The last is not zero, but $1-z$ is itself a zero divisor. In $\mathbb{H}$ the same expression is $1-(-1)=2$, a unit. That gap is precisely what the group ring remembers and $\mathbb{H}$ forgets.

#18

18. Compute $(i+j+k)^2$ in the eight-element basis.

Answer

Nine products, using $i^2=j^2=k^2=z$, $ij=k$, $ji=zk$, $jk=i$, $kj=zi$, $ki=j$, $ik=zj$: $$(i+j+k)^2=3z+(1+z)(i+j+k).$$ Sanity check: multiply by $e_-=\tfrac{1-z}{2}$ and the second term dies, leaving $3z\,e_-=-3e_-$, that is "$(i+j+k)^2=-3$", the expected answer in $\mathbb{H}$.

#19

19. Compute $(2+i-zj)(1+k)$ in full.

Answer

Since $ik=zj$ and $(-zj)k=-z(jk)=-zi$, the $zj$ terms cancel: $$(2+i-zj)(1+k)=2+i-zi+2k.$$ An accident of these coefficients, not a structural fact.

#20

20. Set $e_+=\tfrac{1+z}{2}$ and $e_-=\tfrac{1-z}{2}$. Show each is idempotent, $e_+e_-=0$, $e_++e_-=1$, and both are central. Then compute $z\,e_-$ and $(ie_-)^2$.

Answer

$z$ is central of order $2$, giving $e_\pm^2=e_\pm$ and $e_+e_-=\tfrac{1-z^2}{4}=0$. Then $$z\,e_-=\frac{z-1}{2}=-e_-,\qquad (ie_-)^2=i^2e_-=z\,e_-=-e_-.$$ Inside the ring $\mathbb{R}[Q_8]e_-$, whose identity is $e_-$ rather than $1$, the element $ie_-$ squares to minus the identity. That is how $\mathbb{H}$ hides inside $\mathbb{R}[Q_8]$.

#21

21. Write down the conjugacy classes of $Q_8$ and of $D_4$ and their class sums. Verify by hand that $C=r+r^3\in\mathbb{R}[D_4]$ commutes with both $r$ and $s$.

Answer

$Q_8$: $\{1\},\{z\},\{i,zi\},\{j,zj\},\{k,zk\}$, with sums $1$, $z$, $i+zi$, $j+zj$, $k+zk$.

$D_4$: $\{1\},\{r^2\},\{r,r^3\},\{s,r^2s\},\{rs,r^3s\}$, with sums $1$, $r^2$, $r+r^3$, $s+r^2s$, $rs+r^3s$.

$rC=r^2+r^4=r^2+1=Cr$, and $sC=r^3s+rs=Cs$. Both groups have five classes; hold onto that for #39.

D. Augmentation, involution, center 22–25

#22

22. Define $\varepsilon:\mathbb{R}[G]\to\mathbb{R}$ by $\sum a_gg\mapsto\sum a_g$. (a) Prove it is a ring homomorphism. (b) Show $\ker\varepsilon$ has basis $\{g-1: g\neq 1\}$. (c) Evaluate $\varepsilon$ on your answers to #11 and #19.

Answer

(a) $\varepsilon(\alpha\beta)=\sum_g\sum_{hk=g}a_hb_k=\big(\sum_h a_h\big)\big(\sum_k b_k\big)$, each pair counted once. (b) Each $g-1$ lies in the kernel and they are independent; conversely $\sum a_g=0$ gives $\sum a_gg=\sum_{g\neq 1}a_g(g-1)$. So $\dim\ker\varepsilon=|G|-1$.

(c) $\varepsilon(3+5r-s+r^3s)=8$ and $\varepsilon(2+i-zi+2k)=4$. Cross-check: $\varepsilon(1+2r-s)\,\varepsilon(3+r^3s)=2\cdot 4=8$. ✓

#23

23. (a) If $\alpha$ is a unit, show $\varepsilon(\alpha)\neq 0$. (b) Show the converse fails, using an element from #16. (c) Show $cg$ is a unit for $c\neq 0$ (the "trivial units").

Answer

(a) $\alpha\beta=1$ gives $\varepsilon(\alpha)\varepsilon(\beta)=1$. (b) $\varepsilon(1+z)=2\neq 0$, but $1+z$ is a zero divisor and hence not a unit. (c) $(cg)(c^{-1}g^{-1})=1$. Whether $\mathbb{R}[G]$ has non-trivial units is the interesting question; over $\mathbb{Z}$ it is a whole subject.

#24

24. Define $\alpha^*=\sum a_g\,g^{-1}$. (a) Show $\alpha^{**}=\alpha$ and $(\alpha\beta)^*=\beta^*\alpha^*$. (b) Show the coefficient of $1_G$ in $\alpha\alpha^*$ equals $\sum_g a_g^2$. (c) Conclude that over $\mathbb{R}$, $\alpha\alpha^*=0$ forces $\alpha=0$.

Answer

(a) The coefficient of $g^{-1}$ in $(\alpha\beta)^*$ is $\sum_{hk=g}a_hb_k$, and in $\beta^*\alpha^*$ it is $\sum_{k^{-1}h^{-1}=g^{-1}}b_ka_h$, the same sum. (b) The coefficient of $1$ in $\alpha\alpha^*$ is $\sum_{hk^{-1}=1}a_ha_k=\sum_h a_h^2$. (c) A sum of real squares vanishes only if all terms do.

This is genuinely a fact about $\mathbb{R}$: it fails over $\mathbb{C}$ and over $\mathbb{F}_2$, and that failure is why Maschke needs a characteristic hypothesis.

#25

25. Prove the class sums form an $\mathbb{R}$-basis of $Z(\mathbb{R}[G])$, so $\dim Z(\mathbb{R}[G])$ is the number of conjugacy classes. Confirm this gives $\mathbf{5}$ for both $\mathbb{R}[D_4]$ and $\mathbb{R}[Q_8]$.

Hint

$\alpha$ is central iff $g\alpha g^{-1}=\alpha$ for all $g\in G$ (testing on basis elements suffices). Conjugation permutes the basis, so $\alpha$ is central iff its coefficient function is constant on conjugacy classes.

E. Left multiplication as a linear map 26–31

Left multiplication by a fixed element is a linear transformation, and its spectrum is worth knowing. What follows is the left regular representation; by the end of section H its characteristic polynomial will have told you the difference between $\mathbb{H}$ and $M_2(\mathbb{R})$.

#26

26. For $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}[G]$ define $L_\alpha:\mathbb{R}[G]\to\mathbb{R}[G]$ by $x\mapsto\alpha x$. (a) Show $L_\alpha$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and that $\alpha\mapsto L_\alpha$ is an injective ring homomorphism $\mathbb{R}[G]\to\operatorname{End}_\mathbb{R}(\mathbb{R}[G])\cong M_{|G|}(\mathbb{R})$. (b) Show right multiplication $R_\alpha$ is also linear, but $\alpha\mapsto R_\alpha$ is an anti-homomorphism. Fix it using the involution of #24.

Answer

(a) Linearity and multiplicativity are the distributive and associative laws; injectivity because $L_\alpha(1)=\alpha$. So every group algebra embeds in a matrix algebra, a version of Cayley's theorem one level up.

(b) $R_\alpha R_\beta(x)=(x\beta)\alpha=R_{\beta\alpha}(x)$. The map $\alpha\mapsto R_{\alpha^*}$ is an honest homomorphism.

#27

27. In the ordered basis $(1,r,r^2,r^3,s,rs,r^2s,r^3s)$ of $\mathbb{R}[D_4]$, write the matrices of $L_r$ and $L_s$. Give the cycle structure of each as a permutation, and the characteristic polynomial of each.

Answer

$L_r$ acts as $1\to r\to r^2\to r^3\to 1$ and $s\to rs\to r^2s\to r^3s\to s$: two $4$-cycles, $\chi_{L_r}(t)=(t^4-1)^2$, eigenvalues $1,-1,i,-i$ each with multiplicity $2$.

$L_s$ acts as $1\leftrightarrow s$, $r\leftrightarrow r^3s$, $r^2\leftrightarrow r^2s$, $r^3\leftrightarrow rs$: four transpositions, $\chi_{L_s}(t)=(t^2-1)^4$, eigenvalues $\pm 1$ each with multiplicity $4$.

Both are permutation matrices, which is what left multiplication by a group element always is.

#28

28. Generalize. For $g\in G$ of order $n$, show $L_g$ is a permutation matrix whose cycles all have length $n$, and that there are $|G|/n$ of them. Deduce the characteristic polynomial and the full spectrum with multiplicities. Then compute $\operatorname{tr}(L_g)$ for every $g$.

Answer

Left translation is a free action: $gx=x$ forces $g=1$. So the orbits of $\langle g\rangle$ all have size $n$, giving $|G|/n$ cycles of length $n$. Hence $$\chi_{L_g}(t)=(t^n-1)^{|G|/n},$$ the spectrum is the set of $n$-th roots of unity, each with multiplicity $|G|/n$, and $$\operatorname{tr}(L_g)=\begin{cases}|G| & g=1\\ 0 & g\neq 1.\end{cases}$$ That last line is the regular character, the most useful two-case formula in the subject, and you just proved it by counting fixed points of a permutation.

(b) Compute the rank of $L_e$ for $e=N/8$ in $\mathbb{R}[D_4]$. (c) Compute the rank of $L_{e_-}$ for $e_-=\tfrac{1-z}{2}$ in $\mathbb{R}[Q_8]$. (d) Compute the spectrum of $L_N$.

#31

31. Show that if $\alpha$ is central then $L_\alpha$ commutes with every $L_\beta$ and with every $R_\beta$. Explain in one sentence why this forces $L_\alpha$ to act as a scalar on each irreducible block of the decomposition you will build in section G.

F. The spectrum is a Fourier transform 32–35

For abelian $G$, "diagonalize left multiplication" and "take the Fourier transform" are the same sentence. This is not an analogy.

#32

32. Let $C_n=\langle x\rangle$ and $\alpha=\sum_j a_jx^j$. Show the matrix of $L_\alpha$ in the basis $(1,x,\dots,x^{n-1})$ is the circulant matrix with $(i,k)$ entry $a_{i-k\bmod n}$.

#33

33. Work over $\mathbb{C}$, let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$, and set $f_m=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\omega^{-mj}x^j$. (a) Show $x\cdot f_m=\omega^m f_m$. (b) Deduce $\alpha\cdot f_m=\widehat\alpha(m)\,f_m$ where $\widehat\alpha(m)=\sum_j a_j\omega^{mj}$. (c) Conclude that the spectrum of $L_\alpha$ is exactly the discrete Fourier transform of its coefficient sequence, and that $\det L_\alpha=\prod_m\widehat\alpha(m)$.

Answer

(a) $xf_m=\sum_j\omega^{-mj}x^{j+1}=\sum_l\omega^{-m(l-1)}x^l=\omega^mf_m$. (b) $\alpha f_m=\sum_j a_jx^jf_m=\big(\sum_j a_j\omega^{mj}\big)f_m$. (c) The $f_m$ are $n$ independent eigenvectors, so $L_\alpha$ is diagonalizable with those eigenvalues and the determinant is their product. This is the classical circulant determinant formula, a one-line consequence of group-ring thinking.

#34

34. (a) Show $e_m=f_m/n$ are orthogonal primitive idempotents with $\sum_m e_m=1$. (b) Conclude $\mathbb{C}[C_n]\cong\mathbb{C}^n$ via $\alpha\mapsto(\widehat\alpha(0),\dots,\widehat\alpha(n-1))$, and state what this map does to a product. (c) Write out all four idempotents for $n=4$ explicitly.

Answer

(a) $f_mf_l=\big(\sum_j\omega^{(l-m)j}\big)f_l=n\,\delta_{ml}f_l$. (b) It is a ring isomorphism carrying convolution to pointwise multiplication, the convolution theorem, which here is just "the group ring of a finite abelian group splits along its characters." (c) With $\omega=i$, $$e_m=\tfrac14\big(1+i^{-m}x+i^{-2m}x^2+i^{-3m}x^3\big),\qquad m=0,1,2,3.$$

#35

Route 2: $g^q=g(g^2)^{(q-1)/2}=g\,(p^*)^{(q-1)/2}\equiv g\left(\tfrac{p^*}{q}\right)$ by Euler's criterion.

So $\left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)g\equiv\left(\tfrac{p^*}{q}\right)g\pmod q$. Multiply by $g$ to get $\left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)p^*\equiv\left(\tfrac{p^*}{q}\right)p^*$, and $p^*$ is invertible mod $q$, so $\left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)\equiv\left(\tfrac{p^*}{q}\right)\pmod q$. Both sides are $\pm 1$ and $q>2$, so they are equal.

(g) Using $\left(\tfrac{-1}{q}\right)=(-1)^{(q-1)/2}$, $$\left(\tfrac{p^*}{q}\right)=\left(\tfrac{(-1)^{(p-1)/2}}{q}\right)\left(\tfrac{p}{q}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\frac{q-1}{2}}\left(\tfrac{p}{q}\right),$$ and combining with $\left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)=\left(\tfrac{p^*}{q}\right)$ gives $$\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\cdot\frac{q-1}{2}}.$$ Gauss's theorema aureum, proved by writing down one element of a group ring, squaring it, and reducing it modulo a prime.

L. The corridor: group rings → Laplacians → manifolds and harmonics 50–54

The load-bearing sentence: a group ring is the algebra of translation-invariant operators, and a Laplacian is a translation-invariant operator. Everything from the $n$-cycle to spherical harmonics is that one observation at different levels of generality. Problems 51 and 52 are hand computations; 53 and 54 are the doorway.

#50

50. Reinterpret $\mathbb{C}[G]$ as the space of functions $G\to\mathbb{C}$ under convolution. Prove that a linear operator $T$ on $\mathbb{C}[G]$ commutes with every right translation $R_h$ if and only if $T=L_\alpha$ for a unique $\alpha$. So $\mathbb{C}[G]$ is the algebra of translation-invariant operators.

Answer

One direction is associativity. For the other, put $\alpha=T(1)$; then for any $h$, $T(h)=T(R_h1)=R_hT(1)=\alpha h$, so $T=L_\alpha$ by linearity, and uniqueness holds because $L_\alpha(1)=\alpha$.

This one lemma is the corridor. Replace "finite group with counting measure" by "compact group with Haar measure" and it still holds; replace it by "$\mathbb{R}^n$ under translation" and translation-invariant operators are exactly convolution operators, that is, Fourier multipliers, which is why constant-coefficient differential operators (the Laplacian foremost) are diagonalized by the Fourier transform.

54. The far end of the corridor: spherical harmonics. Let $S^2=SO(3)/SO(2)$.

(a) Verify that $x,y,z$ are harmonic homogeneous polynomials on $\mathbb{R}^3$, that $SO(3)$ preserves their span, and that their restrictions to $S^2$ satisfy $\Delta_{S^2}Y=-2Y$.

(b) Let $H_\ell$ be the harmonic homogeneous polynomials of degree $\ell$ in three variables. Compute $\dim H_\ell$ for $\ell=0,1,2,3$ by hand, and prove $\dim H_\ell=2\ell+1$ in general.

(c) State why $\Delta_{S^2}$ acts as a scalar on each $H_\ell$, citing the exact problem on this page that is doing the work.

(d) We have $L^2(S^2)=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{\ell\ge0}H_\ell$, with $H_\ell$ the $(2\ell+1)$-dimensional irreducible representation of $SO(3)$. Explain why every odd-dimensional irreducible appears exactly once, and why the even-dimensional ones do not appear at all.

Hint

(c) It is #31 verbatim, for a compact group: an operator commuting with an irreducible action is a scalar. Which action does $\Delta_{S^2}$ commute with, and why? (d) Functions on $G/K$ are functions on $G$ invariant under right $K$-translation, so an irreducible $V_\pi$ contributes $\dim V_\pi^K$ copies. Then ask what $SO(2)$-fixed vectors look like in each $SO(3)$-irrep, and remember that the even-dimensional ones are honest representations of $SU(2)$ but not of $SO(3)$.

Answer

(a) $\Delta x=0$ trivially; $SO(3)$ acts by linear substitution, preserving degree, and commutes with $\Delta$ because $\Delta$ is rotation-invariant. For a harmonic homogeneous $p$ of degree $\ell$, separating variables in $\Delta=\partial_r^2+\tfrac{2}{r}\partial_r+r^{-2}\Delta_{S^2}$ applied to $r^\ell Y$ gives $\Delta_{S^2}Y=-\ell(\ell+1)Y$; at $\ell=1$ that is $-2$.

(b) Homogeneous degree-$\ell$ polynomials in three variables have dimension $\binom{\ell+2}{2}$, and $\Delta$ maps them onto degree $\ell-2$, so $$\dim H_\ell=\binom{\ell+2}{2}-\binom{\ell}{2}=2\ell+1,$$ giving $1,3,5,7$ for $\ell=0,1,2,3$.

(c) #31. $\Delta_{S^2}$ commutes with the $SO(3)$-action by rotation invariance, so it is an intertwiner of an irreducible representation, hence a scalar by Schur. In group-ring language: $\Delta$ is a central element of the convolution algebra, and central elements act by scalars on each isotypic block, exactly as $N$ did in #30(d) and as the class sums do in #25.

(d) $L^2(S^2)=L^2(SO(3)/SO(2))=L^2(SO(3))^{SO(2)}$, so by Peter–Weyl each irreducible $V_\pi$ appears with multiplicity $\dim V_\pi^{SO(2)}$. In the $(2\ell+1)$-dimensional irrep the $SO(2)$-weights are $-\ell,\dots,0,\dots,\ell$: exactly one zero weight, so exactly one fixed vector, so multiplicity one. The even-dimensional irreps have half-integer weights, no zero weight and no fixed vector, and in fact they are representations of $SU(2)$ that do not descend to $SO(3)$ at all. Multiplicity zero.

So the decomposition of $L^2(S^2)$ into spherical harmonics, the eigenvalues $-\ell(\ell+1)$, and the degeneracy $2\ell+1$ of each energy level — the structure that gives the periodic table its shape — is the same theorem as $\mathbb{C}[D_4]\cong\mathbb{C}^4\times M_2(\mathbb{C})$. One is finite and can be done by hand in an evening; the other is compact and needs an integral. The algebra is identical.

Where the rooms meet. The through-line is a single move: a group ring turns a group into a linear-algebra problem, and its natural coordinates are characters. In section F those characters are the DFT and the coordinates diagonalize convolution. In section H the failure to fully diagonalize over $\mathbb{R}$ is a quadratic form, and the classification of quadratic forms is the classification of the algebra. In section J the characters live in a Galois field and the Frobenius orbits control everything. In section K the character is the Legendre symbol, its Fourier coefficient is a Gauss sum, the Galois group acts on that sum by that same character, and reduction mod $q$ closes the loop. In section L the group is no longer finite: the same characters become Fourier modes, the same central elements become Laplacians, and the same block decomposition becomes Peter–Weyl.
